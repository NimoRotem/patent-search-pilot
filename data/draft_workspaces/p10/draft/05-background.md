Temperature-sensitive goods such as vaccines, biological samples, prepared meals and laboratory reagents are frequently shipped in insulated carriers that hold a payload within a target temperature band for a period of hours or days. Carriers of this general kind fall into two broad families. Powered carriers use a fan, a pump or another driven air mover, together with a power source, to force air over a cold source and through the payload space. Passive carriers omit the power source and rely on conduction from, and natural convection around, a refrigerant mass such as water ice, dry ice, gel packs or sealed cassettes charged with a phase-change material.

Passive carriers are attractive because they need have no battery to charge, no motor to fail and no electrical approval burden for air transport. They are also harder to control. When a refrigerant mass is simply laid on top of, beside or beneath a payload, the cooled air falls where gravity and the packing arrangement happen to send it. Items directly against the refrigerant are driven toward the cold end of the acceptable band or below it, while items on the far side of the payload sit in a stagnant warm layer. The result can be a temperature spread across the payload that tends to grow as the load is packed more densely, and that may vary from shipment to shipment because it depends on how a particular packer arranged the goods and the refrigerant on a particular day.

Attempts to impose a defined circulation path on a passive carrier run into a practical difficulty. The path is defined by clearances, by openings and by the alignment between them, and every one of those relationships is destroyed if the component that carries the openings is set down rotated, offset or skewed by a person working quickly at a packing bench. A carrier that circulates air well when it is assembled precisely, and poorly when it is not, delivers the variability it was intended to remove. Packing instructions, printed arrows and keyed corner blocks all depend on the packer looking, and none of them acts on a component that has already been set down out of position.

Two further problems attend repeated reuse of a passive carrier. First, moist ambient air admitted each time the carrier is opened condenses on the cold surfaces of the refrigerant mass and runs down into the payload space, wetting labels and cartons and, over many cycles, leaving standing water in the base of the carrier. Where the refrigerant is a sealed cassette, drying the interior often means removing the cassette from service. Second, a passive carrier gives no account of itself. If the payload was exposed to a temperature excursion in transit, and the refrigerant has since recovered, an inspection at the receiving dock shows a cold carrier and a firm cassette, and the excursion leaves no trace. Electronic data loggers can record such events, but add cost, battery management and a reading step to every unit shipped, and they report a sensor location rather than the state of the refrigerant that the payload actually depends on.

There remains a need for a reusable passive carrier that establishes the same circulation path around the payload every time it is closed, without a powered air mover and without relying on the care of the person who packed it, that manages the condensate produced by repeated opening without taking the refrigerant out of service, and that retains a visible mechanical record of a refrigerant temperature excursion after the refrigerant has recovered.
