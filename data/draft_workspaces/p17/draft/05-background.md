Mounting a pull on a cabinet door or a drawer front requires two holes that satisfy three separate conditions at once. The two holes must lie at a common setback from a straight edge of the workpiece, they must be separated by the center-to-center spacing of the particular pull being installed, and the pair must sit symmetrically about a chosen centerline of the workpiece. A hole that is correct in one of these three respects and wrong in another still spoils a finished panel, and a panel that has been drilled cannot be corrected by moving the hole.

Laying the holes out by hand with a rule and a square requires the installer to compute and mark two positions for every panel, to transfer each mark accurately, and then to start a drill bit exactly on a pencil intersection on a hard and often prefinished surface. Small errors in measuring, in marking, and in bit wander accumulate independently on each of the two holes, so the pair can be out of square with the edge even when each individual measurement was made carefully. Repeating the layout on every door and drawer front of a run multiplies both the labor and the opportunities for error.

Simple drilling guides reduce part of this burden by presenting a hardened bushing that both positions the bit and keeps it perpendicular to the surface. A guide of that kind still leaves the installer to establish where the guide itself is placed on the panel, and a guide that fixes the two bushings at a single separation serves only one pull pattern. Adjustable arrangements in which a scale is read once and a second hole is measured from the first reintroduce the accumulation of error that the guide was meant to remove, because an error in setting the single reading displaces both holes and an error in transferring the spacing displaces only one of them.

A further difficulty arises from the direction in which the workpiece is referenced. A pull on an upper cabinet door is commonly located from the lower edge of the door, while a pull on a drawer front or a base cabinet door is commonly located from the upper edge or from a stile edge on the opposite side. A jig whose edge fence projects from one longitudinal side of its body can be referenced from the opposite edge only by turning the whole jig over, which puts the marked and readable face of the jig against the panel where it cannot be seen, or by printing a second set of marks that runs in the opposite direction, which invites the installer to read the wrong scale. Neither expedient is satisfactory in the field, where the installer moves repeatedly between doors and drawer fronts on the same run of cabinets.

Powered and electronically assisted layout systems can measure and record positions, but they add cost, require charging or batteries, and are unnecessary for an operation whose entire geometry is fixed by one straight edge, one centerline, and one spacing dimension.
