Parking structures, fleet depots, workplaces and multi unit dwellings commonly install several electric vehicle connectors on a single electrical branch. The conductors, overcurrent device and upstream transformer capacity of that branch are sized for an aggregate current that is lower, and often much lower, than the sum of the individual current ratings of the connectors served by it. The installation therefore depends on a control function that continuously divides the branch capacity among the connectors that are in use, rather than on the physical rating of any one connector.

A control function of this kind ordinarily issues a current limit to each vehicle over a control pilot signalling interface. The limit is advisory in the sense that it is a request addressed to equipment that the site operator does not own and cannot inspect. A vehicle may respond slowly, may respond only in coarse steps, may hold a previous current for an interval after a new limit is signalled, or may not follow the signalled limit at all. Auxiliary equipment on the same branch, such as lighting, ventilation, elevators or refrigeration, draws current that the charging control function does not command and that changes without notice. A control function that computes an allocation and then assumes the allocation has taken effect can therefore hold the branch above its rated aggregate current while every connector believes it is compliant, with the overcurrent device of the branch as the only remaining protection.

Site controllers of this class also depend on a network connection to a remote management service for schedules, tariffs and driver authorization. Connectivity in a below grade parking structure or a remote depot is intermittent. A controller that treats loss of connectivity as a reason to stop charging strands vehicles that were already authorized, while a controller that treats loss of connectivity as permission to charge without restraint abandons the branch limit that the last accepted configuration established, and abandons the accounting record that billing depends on.

A further difficulty arises at installation. The connector identifier used by the control function, the physical contactor that energizes a connector and the current sensing channel that measures it are joined by field wiring performed once, often by a contractor who does not operate the equipment afterwards. If that wiring map is recorded incorrectly, the control function will deliver a computed current limit to one connector while measuring another. Every subsequent verification appears to succeed, because the two errors are consistent with one another, and the fault is discovered only when the branch overcurrent device operates or when a vehicle receives a limit that was intended for a different vehicle.

There remains a need for a controller that treats the aggregate current of the branch as a measured quantity, verified independently of the per connector measurements and independently of any vehicle response, and that converts an unanswered current reduction into a bounded, ordered and recoverable removal of load rather than into an uncontrolled trip of the branch overcurrent device.
