Here is my invention.

The present invention relates to a vibration device for setting tiles and other covering materials into thinset, mortar, adhesive, or similar bonding material. Conventional tile vibrators generally include a vibrating motor connected to a handle and either a smooth sliding base or a flexible suction cup. The vibration is intended to remove trapped air, distribute the bonding material, improve surface contact, and strengthen the bond between the tile and the underlying substrate.

Existing systems have two main disadvantages. First, vibration from the motor is not efficiently coupled to the tile. A smooth sliding base must be pressed firmly against the tile by the operator, and even then a thin layer of air between the base and the tile may dampen vibration transfer. A suction-cup system creates a stronger connection, but the flexible rubber or foam forming the suction cup absorbs and dampens part of the vibration. A conventional suction cup also attaches the machine at one fixed location, preventing it from sliding across the tile and causing vibration intensity to decrease at areas farther from the attachment point.

Second, existing systems require the operator to continuously apply substantial downward pressure to maintain contact with the tile. This transfers vibration into the operator’s hands, wrists, arms, and joints, creating discomfort, fatigue, and possible injury. Shock absorbers and vibration-damping handles may reduce some vibration reaching the operator, but they also absorb mechanical energy that could otherwise be transferred to the tile. Furthermore, stronger coupling generally requires greater hand pressure, which increases the vibration experienced by the operator.

The invention addresses these problems by providing a vibration motor coupled to a first side of a rigid base. An air-extraction mechanism, such as a vacuum pump, is also coupled to the first side and is in fluid communication with a chamber located on a second side of the base. The perimeter of the second side is formed as a rigid loop or skirt made from a low-friction material such as plastic, metal, PTFE, or a combination of rigid materials.

Unlike conventional vacuum grippers or suction cups, the rigid perimeter is not intended to form a complete airtight seal and does not rely on flexible rubber or foam. Instead, it restricts airflow into the chamber while maintaining rigid contact with the tile. The air-extraction mechanism continuously removes air entering beneath the rigid perimeter, maintaining the chamber at a lower pressure than the surrounding atmosphere. The resulting pressure differential holds the device firmly against the tile without requiring the operator to press downward.

Because the vibration motor is connected to the tile through rigid components, vibration energy is transferred with substantially less damping than through a flexible suction cup. At the same time, the low-friction perimeter allows the device to slide freely across different areas of the tile while the vacuum continuously maintains contact.

The device may include one or more handles used primarily for guiding the machine rather than pressing it against the surface. In another embodiment, a rope, wire, cable, or similar flexible pulling element is connected to the device. Pulling the flexible element moves the device across the tile, while the pressure differential independently supplies the force holding the device against the surface. This provides decoupled control in which the operator controls position using minimal pulling force while the vacuum maintains consistent coupling and vibration transfer. The invention therefore maximizes vibration delivered to the tile while minimizing vibration, pressure, fatigue, and joint stress experienced by the operator.
