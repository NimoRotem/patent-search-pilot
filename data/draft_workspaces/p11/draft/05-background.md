Sites that install several electric-vehicle charging connectors frequently supply those connectors from a single electrical branch. The branch is protected at an allowable aggregate current, referred to herein as a branch limit, that is commonly lower than the sum of the individual connector ratings. Installing a branch large enough for every connector to draw its full rating at the same time is often impractical.

Charging equipment therefore has to share a fixed amount of current among connectors that could collectively demand more. The signalling mechanism ordinarily available for that purpose is the control pilot presented to each vehicle, which advertises a maximum current that the vehicle is permitted to draw. Control-pilot signalling is advisory in nature. The value advertised on the pilot is a command, not a measurement, and the current actually drawn depends on the behaviour of the vehicle receiving it. A vehicle may respond slowly, may respond only in coarse steps, may hold a previous value, or may not reduce at all. Control that assumes the commanded value is the delivered value therefore has no way to detect the case in which the branch remains loaded above its limit after a reduction has been ordered, and that case is precisely the one in which the branch protective device is at risk of operating and removing service from every connector on the branch.

A further difficulty arises because the branch usually carries loads other than charging. Equipment on the same branch other than the charging connectors consumes part of the allowable aggregate current, and that consumption varies through the day. Allocation that divides the full branch limit among the connectors, without accounting for the varying non-charging portion of the branch load, either overcommits the branch or forces the installer to configure a permanently conservative limit that wastes capacity for most of the day.

Sharing schemes that divide available current equally, or in proportion to instantaneous request, also tend to treat every session alike. Where sessions have been served unequally over the course of a shared-capacity period, for example because one session began while the branch was heavily loaded and another began while it was lightly loaded, equal division perpetuates the inequality rather than correcting it.

Charging equipment is additionally exposed to conditions that are specific to its power path and its installation. A contactor whose contacts have welded continues to pass current after an open command has been issued, so an allocation scheme that treats an open command as removal of the load will continue to believe that capacity has been released when it has not. Site communication to a remote management system is not continuous, so equipment that depends on a remote authority for both authorization and current limits either stops serving vehicles when the link fails or continues without a governing limit. Multi-connector equipment is also wired by hand, and a transposition between the logical identifier of a connector and the physical contactor and sensor channel that serve it causes a current limit intended for one connector to be applied to another, which defeats the allocation entirely while leaving every individual component apparently functional.

There remains a need for charging control that treats the current actually present on the branch and at each connector as the governing quantity, that keeps the branch within its limit even when a vehicle does not honour a reduced pilot command, that allocates the genuinely available portion of the branch limit in a way that corrects accumulated inequality between sessions, and that remains safe and operable when the site link is down, when a contactor has welded, and when the installation wiring map has not been independently confirmed.
