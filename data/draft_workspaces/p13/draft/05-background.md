Heavy machines are frequently installed at a standoff height, standing clear of the roof, floor, vehicle bed, or other support surface that carries them. When the required standoff height is not known until the machine is in place, or when the height has to change later, a support of fixed height is inconvenient, because setting or resetting the height means lifting the machine off the support again.

A height-adjustable platform avoids that lifting, but it introduces three problems that a design must solve together before such a platform can carry a heavy machine dependably.

The first problem is parallelism. If each corner of a deck is adjusted independently of the others, the corners are adjusted one after another. The deck twists and cocks in its guides while the adjustment is in progress, and the finished height at one corner differs from the finished height at another, so that the machine is left out of level.

The second problem is load holding. A drive train that both moves the deck and holds it is a single load path. Its gearing, its fasteners, and its input coupling all have to carry the standing weight of the machine for as long as the machine is installed, and any wear, backlash, or maintenance operation on that drive train is a wear, backlash, or maintenance operation on the only thing supporting the load.

The third problem is the state of the locks. Where a platform is held by separate mechanical locks, those locks must be released to adjust the height and must be returned afterwards. Two failure modes follow. A lock that is only partly released is dragged across the toothed member it engages while the platform is being adjusted, and a lock that is never returned leaves the platform standing on its drive train while looking, from the outside, exactly like a platform that is locked. Where several locks are pulled by one linkage through separate flexible connections, ordinary differences in connection length can leave one lock engaged while the other locks are released, because the shortest connection lifts its lock first and the longest connection may not lift its lock at all.

There remains a need for a reusable height-adjustable equipment platform that changes the height of every post of a deck by the same distance from a single input, that holds the resulting height through load locks that are independent of the drive train, that releases every one of those load locks together despite differences in the lengths of the flexible connections that pull them, and that shows the operator, before the platform is loaded, that every load lock has in fact returned.
