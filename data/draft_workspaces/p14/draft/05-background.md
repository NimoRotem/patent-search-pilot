Temperature-sensitive payloads such as medicines, diagnostic samples, and perishable foods are frequently distributed in insulated containers that carry their own thermal reserve. A container of this kind is loaded at a packing point, handed to one or more couriers, and opened at a destination that may be many hours away. Because the container travels unattended and often without electrical power, the thermal behaviour of the container has to be set at the moment of closing and cannot be corrected in transit.

Two practical difficulties dominate the use of such containers. The first is the distribution of cold within the payload space. A thermal reserve placed against one wall, or laid directly on top of a payload, cools the material nearest to it strongly and the material farthest from it weakly, so the payload experiences a spread of temperatures. Direct contact between the thermal reserve and the payload can drive the contacted items below the intended range while more distant items drift above it. Moving air distributes the effect of the reserve more evenly, but a fan, pump, or other powered air mover adds a battery, a failure mode, and a service burden, and is unwelcome in a container that has to be returned and used again.

The second difficulty is that a container which relies on an internal air path only performs as intended if that path is actually present when the container is closed. Loose internal components are lifted, rotated, or nudged during loading and during transport. If the passages that are supposed to carry conditioned air downward are displaced relative to the passages that are supposed to carry it back, the intended path is broken and the container behaves as an ordinary insulated box, without any outward sign that anything is wrong. Placing that responsibility on the person who packs the container is unreliable, because a component that is merely set in place looks the same whether or not it is correctly positioned.

Related handling problems arise in reusable service. Condensate forms on cold surfaces inside a closed container and collects where it is difficult to reach. Removing it commonly means handling the thermal reserve itself, which is undesirable when the reserve is a phase-change cassette that should not be opened in the field. Finally, a payload that has been briefly warmed can arrive at a temperature that appears correct, so a recipient inspecting the container at delivery cannot tell from the contents alone whether the thermal reserve lost its condition earlier in the journey.

There remains a need for a reusable passive carrier in which the internal circulation path is established positively by the act of closing the container, in which the thermal reserve is held in that established relationship during transport, and in which routine servicing and evidence of thermal exposure are available without opening the thermal reserve.
