Many liquid handling installations add a small, accurately measured quantity of a concentrate to a much larger stream of a carrier liquid that is already flowing in a pipe. The concentrate may be aggressive, staining, strongly colored, biologically active, or simply expensive, and the quantity that must be added is small in comparison with the carrier flow. The addition therefore has to be made repeatably, without measuring the concentrate by eye, and without allowing the concentrate to reach any part of the installation other than the carrier stream itself.

Two practical difficulties dominate this kind of dosing. The first is separation of the two liquids upstream of the point of addition. Where a dosing device sits between a concentrate source and a carrier line under pressure, a path that momentarily connects the two can allow carrier liquid to be forced back toward the concentrate source, or can allow concentrate to reach the carrier line at a time when no dose is called for. Where the two sides are kept apart by separately actuated shut-off elements, the separation depends on those elements being commanded in the correct order.

The second difficulty is residue. The surfaces that the concentrate touches, in particular the port through which it enters the metering volume and the seat of the check element through which the dose leaves, may retain a film of concentrate after the dose has been delivered. Where such a film passes into the carrier stream between doses, the quantity actually added is not the quantity that was measured; and where it dries or reacts on the wetted surfaces, the accuracy of the valve changes in service. Where the valve must be broken out of the line and cleaned by hand, the installation is taken out of service; and where a rinse is a separate operation commanded after the dose, it depends on that operation being performed at the right moment.

Dose selection presents a further difficulty. Adjustable dosing devices may be set by a screw or by an electronically stored value. There remains a need for a setting that is retained by the mechanism itself and that is visible at the valve without electronics.

There remains a need for an inline metering valve in which the separation of the concentrate from the carrier liquid is enforced by the geometry of the moving parts rather than by the order in which external commands arrive, in which the surfaces wetted by the concentrate are rinsed by the carrier liquid as an unavoidable part of the return of the metering element, and in which the selected dose is both retained and displayed mechanically.
