A cordless handheld vacuum lifter is used to move glass panes, stone slabs, and similar flat panels on site by suction gripping. A suction cup is pressed against the face of the panel, air is removed from the space enclosed between the cup and the panel, and the resulting difference between atmospheric pressure and the pressure in that enclosed space clamps the cup to the panel so that the panel can be carried.

A suction grip is only as good as its seal. Dust on the panel, a textured or porous surface, a nick in the sealing lip, a change in temperature, or simply the slow permeation and leakage that occurs over minutes of handling will all bleed air back into the enclosed space. As air returns, the clamping force falls. The failure is progressive and, from the point of view of the person holding the panel, silent: nothing about the feel of the tool changes until the cup lets go, at which point a heavy panel is already falling.

Vacuum lifting equipment has therefore long been fitted with vacuum monitoring. In one known lifter for transporting loads having an adhesion surface, a suction plate assembly carries projecting annular seals defining a vacuum chamber connected to a main vacuum source, a battery-fed emergency vacuum source that can be started by an emergency switch is additionally provided, and a pressure pickup associated with the vacuum chamber is coupled with a malfunction alarm such that the alarm is turned on when a preset limit pressure is exceeded during vacuum operation [REF:US-5310232-A]. That equipment is a suction plate assembly with a main vacuum source and a separate emergency vacuum source; the alarm is described as a malfunction alarm tied to a preset limit pressure, and the reference does not describe the limit pressure in terms of the vacuum still needed to keep hold of the load.

Control systems for vacuum lift equipment have also been described in which a programmable controller interprets feedback from vacuum level sensors and battery sensors and responsively provides warnings and limits or alters machine functions, including evaluating a change in measured vacuum level over time to determine a rate of vacuum level change that may be caused by a leak and outputting a warning indication when that rate exceeds a threshold rate [REF:US-10518985-B2]. Such a system is built around a controller with memory, stored records, and machine-function interlocks, and its leak warning is a response to how fast the vacuum is changing rather than to where the vacuum sits relative to the point at which the grip would fail.

Powered suction devices in a hand-carried format are also known. An electric vacuum suction lifter has been described having a main body with a suction surface carrying first and second annular sealing units that bound a sealed space, a control unit, a vacuum pump controlled by the control unit to draw air from the sealed space, a pressure sensing unit that measures the pressure of the sealed space to generate instant pressure information, a battery unit that is charged by external power equipment and powers the control unit, pump, and pressure sensing unit, a start button, a pressure relief button, and at least one handle by which a member gripped by the device can be carried [REF:US-10625955-B2]. In that device the instant pressure information is compared with a high predetermined pressure and a low predetermined pressure: reaching the high predetermined pressure stops the pump, and falling below the low predetermined pressure restarts the pump. A warning unit is described as being operated when the detected battery power is below a predetermined battery power, and warning information relating to pressure is described as being transmitted to an external electronic device. A closely similar electric suction cup has likewise been described, in which the pressure detection unit detects the pressure between the suction cup and the component in real time and the vacuum pump automatically acts again when that pressure is lower than a second predetermined pressure [REF:CN-110630621-B].

Other hand-carried powered suction devices have likewise been described. A cordless wand device for grasping objects such as silicon wafers has a housing with a hollow interior, a tip attached to the housing through a hollow tube and having a perimeter region contoured to form an airtight seal when placed against the surface of the object, a power source and a vacuum pump positioned within the housing, a switch by which the pump is turned on and off, and light-emitting diodes of different colours which indicate to an operator whether the power source is sufficiently charged to generate sufficient suction force to safely grasp the object, the sufficiency being determined from the voltage drop across a resistor network fed by the power source [REF:US-5374090-A]. What is indicated there is the state of the power source and the sufficiency of the suction force for taking hold, not how much hold remains on an object already being carried.

A portable electric carrying suction cup structure has been described in which a handle is fixed to the upper end of a suction cup body and a vacuum pump is provided on the suction cup body, with a control unit connected between the pump and the power supply part, so that air is pumped out from between the suction cup body and the surface of a smooth material and is continuously pumped out after an airtight state has been reached in order to avoid loosening and falling during movement; the control unit is provided with a pressure gauge by which the user can know the pressure change, with a light source displaying different usage states, and with a buzzer for sending out alarm sounds according to different usage states [REF:CN-2806399-Y]. Here again the answer to a decaying grip is to keep the pump running, and what the buzzer is tied to is a usage state rather than the amount of grip left.

A digital display suction cup has been described comprising a suction cup and a handle structure, the two ends of the handle structure carrying a switch button and a pressure relief valve, the handle structure carrying a battery and an electric vacuum pump connected to the suction cup through an air pipe, and the lower part of the battery end carrying an air pressure sensor and a display screen which displays the negative pressure value of the suction cup and the battery power, the battery being charged through a USB charging port [REF:US-2024424646-A1]. That device puts a number in front of the user and leaves the reading of the number, and the judgement of what it means for the panel in hand, to the user.

A portable hand grab has been described including one or more suction cups, at least one handle connected to the suction cups, at least one electrical vacuum pump for at least partially evacuating a suction volume between the suction cups and the attachment surfaces, at least one vacuum sensor for detecting a vacuum level in the suction volume, a power storage unit, and an electronic controller configured to control the pump to evacuate the suction volume when the vacuum sensor detects the vacuum level being less than a preselected minimum vacuum level, together with an attach indicator which the controller activates when the vacuum sensor detects the vacuum level being greater than a preselected safe vacuum level [REF:WO-2017156581-A1]. The indicator there reports that attachment has been achieved, being activated above a preselected safe vacuum level, and the response to a vacuum level below the preselected minimum is once more to run the pump.

A sucker for plate glass has also been described in which the sucker body defines a suction hole at its suction surface and carries a manually operated vacuum pump, a sensor communicating with the suction channel that connects the two suction holes and including a piston lever which moves in proportion to the degree of vacuum, a switch closed by the piston lever when the degree of vacuum reaches a predetermined value, a power source, and a sound generator which generates sound when the switch is closed [REF:US-5184858-A]. The pump in that sucker is worked by hand, and the sound is generated when the sensor detects a predetermined degree of vacuum, the passages of record describing that value only as a predetermined value.

Multi-cup handling equipment has been described in which two or more vacuum modules are mounted on a mounting frame, each module having a cup, an electrically powered vacuum pump, a fluid circuit, a controller, and optionally a pressure sensor and a battery, with an interface through which a user selects an operational mode of the modules, and in which each module may include indicators that indicate a functional condition of the module visually or audibly [REF:WO-2022236379-A1].

Two shortcomings persist for a person who is holding a panel in one hand. First, the response to a decaying grip in the known powered devices is to run the pump again: the device treats a falling vacuum as a housekeeping event to be corrected, and neither [REF:US-10625955-B2] nor [REF:CN-110630621-B] relates the low predetermined pressure at which it acts to the vacuum still needed to hold the load. If the leak is larger than the pump can make up - a torn lip, a dusty or porous panel, a cracked substrate - the pump simply runs while the grip continues to decay, and any signal that is given is tied to the low predetermined pressure at which the pump is restarted, or is transmitted to an external electronic device, rather than to the vacuum still needed to hold the load. Second, where a signal is given at all, it is given on a condition unrelated to the hold on the load (battery state or charge sufficiency), on a rate of change, on a usage state, on the achievement of attachment above a safe level, or at a preset or predetermined value whose relationship to the load-holding capacity of the cup is not established. None of these guarantees the one thing a person carrying a slab actually needs: notice that arrives while the cup is still holding, with grip left over to get the panel down.

There remains a need for a self-contained, cordless, hand-held vacuum lifter that tells the user that the grip is failing at a point on the decay curve at which the grip has not yet failed.
